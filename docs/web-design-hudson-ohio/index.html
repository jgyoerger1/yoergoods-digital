The Hudson project: All Paws Pet Care

Emily and Baylie are sisters-in-law who run a pet sitting business in Hudson: sitting, dog walking and boarding. Nothing about the business needed fixing. It had repeat clients, a five-star rating and a habit of sending photo updates with each visit.

The missing piece was a front door. The only place to send a curious neighbor was a Rover profile inside an app. They wanted one link that explains the services in about thirty seconds and makes booking a single tap, and they wanted it fast and almost free to run.

What the one page does

  • It is built for a phone first. Services, who they are, reviews and common questions sit in one scroll, because a pet owner looking for a sitter in the evening is holding a phone.
  • It sends every booking to Rover. Every button on the page goes to their Rover profile. Rover keeps handling the calendar, the payments and the insurance, so there is no second booking system to keep up.
  • It lets the reviews do the selling. The quotes and numbers on the page are real, taken from their Rover profile.
  • It shows their own animals. Twenty photos of real dogs and cats from their Facebook and Instagram feeds, resized and captioned. No stock photos.
  • It costs nothing to host. It is a static page with a one-click publish script, with nothing to renew and nothing to update.

One page or more

When one page is enough, and when it is not

A one-page website is not a small version of a big one. It is the right tool for one kind of business and the wrong tool for another. Here is how I tell them apart.

One page is enough when

The business already works and needs a front door.

  • You do one main thing, or a short list of closely related things.
  • Customers mostly need to know what you do, where you work and how to reach you.
  • Booking or payment already happens somewhere else: an app, a phone call, a message.
  • Your proof is reviews and photos that fit in one scroll.

A sitter, a tutor, a baker working from home or a one-person trade often fits.

You need more pages when

People search for each thing you do separately.

  • You offer several distinct services and want each one found on its own.
  • You have enough finished work to fill a gallery.
  • Customers need to send you photos or details before you can quote.

How a one-page project starts

Write the page down in one sentence first.

Before any design, the All Paws scope fit in one line: one page, all roads lead to Rover, live today. Anything that did not serve that line stayed out. That discipline is why the page could go from idea to live in one day and still feel complete. Four questions get you to your own sentence.

01

What should a visitor do?

Pick one action. For All Paws it was book on Rover. For another business it might be call, text or ask for a quote.

02

Where is the proof?

Reviews you have already earned and photos you have already taken. A reviews section can use what you have on Google, Rover, Yelp or in your inbox.

03

What stays where it is?

If an app or a phone call already handles booking and payment, the page points there. It does not replace it.

04

What gets left out?

Anything that does not serve the sentence. It can be added later if the business grows into it.
