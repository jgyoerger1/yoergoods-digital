The brief

Every hunting app I tried either charged a subscription for the basics or buried them under a social feed. I wanted four things in one fast app: my stands on a map and which ones the wind allows today, an honest forecast of when deer will be on their feet, a way to follow a blood trail in bad light, and the property lines so I know whose door to knock on. It had to work in the woods with one bar of signal, and keep my spots on my phone unless I choose to share them.

What got built

  • A stand map that reads the wind. Drop pins for stands, blinds, cameras, scrapes and trails. Each stand knows which winds work for it and draws a scent cone from the live wind, so one glance tells you which stand to hunt this afternoon.
  • HuntCast, an explained forecast. A 0 to 100 movement score for every hour of the next week, built from barometric pressure, fronts, temperature drops, wind, rain and the rut calendar for your latitude. Every plus and minus is spelled out in plain English, and the app says honestly which factors the research supports and which it doesn't.
  • A blood-trail camera. The phone camera grays out everything except blood-red pixels in real time, with modes for low light and dark soil, and a "mark blood here" pin that drops onto the map.
  • Property lines, owners and public land. Parcel boundaries in 31 states, owner names in 20 where counties publish them, public hunting land and game units, and a permission-letter generator for the landowner you want to ask.
  • Terrain from survey data. Hillshade, slope and 5-foot contours from the U.S. Geological Survey's elevation service, so pinch points and benches stand out.
  • An illustrated Field Guide. Scroll-driven lessons on field dressing, reading a blood trail and scoring a buck, with a score sheet that does the arithmetic and a tool that measures a rack from a photo.
  • Private by default, shareable on purpose. Pins and photos live on the phone. An optional account syncs them across devices and shares chosen pins with your hunting camp.

The project manager part

Rutline was built in focused sessions, each with one written goal and a test before moving on: the wind cone, then the forecast, then the camera, then property lines. Scope was guarded the same way it is on client work. When a feature misbehaved under real load, like a property-line request that tried to draw thirty thousand parcels at once, the fix was a written limit, not a bigger phone. The forecast's honesty note came from the same habit: say what the evidence supports, and nothing more.

Details that matter

  • Installs from the browser to the home screen and keeps working offline after the first load. No ads, no subscription.
  • One accent color, ember orange, on charcoal, so the map stays readable in a tree stand at first light.
  • Motion with a job: pins drop, the wind streaks, the forecast draws in. All of it switches off for anyone who prefers reduced motion.
  • The iPhone App Store build is wired up and waiting on a developer account.
