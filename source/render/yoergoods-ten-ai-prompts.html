Ten AI prompts that pay for themselves at work

Paste into Microsoft 365 Copilot, Claude or ChatGPT. Replace the [brackets]. Always read the answer before you send it anywhere.

01Meeting notes into minutes

Here are my raw notes from a meeting: [paste]. Turn them into minutes with three sections: Decisions, Actions (owner, due date, one line each), Open questions. Keep every name. If something is unclear, list it under Open questions instead of guessing.

Why it works: it names the structure and bans guessing, which is where meeting summaries usually go wrong.

02The five-line status

Turn these updates into a status note for an executive who has 30 seconds: [paste]. Five lines max: what changed this week, what is at risk, what I need from them, next milestone and date, one-word RAG rating with a reason.

Why it works: executives read for risk and asks. This puts both above the fold.

03Inbox triage and reply

Summarize this email thread in three bullets, tell me exactly what is being asked of me and by when, then draft a reply in my voice: short, direct, friendly, no corporate filler. Thread: [paste].

Why it works: separating "what is being asked" from the draft stops you answering the wrong question well.

04Deck outline for a decision

I need a six-slide deck to get approval for [decision] from [audience]. Give me a slide title that states the point for each slide, one supporting message per slide, and two lines of speaker notes. Slide 6 is the ask with options.

Why it works: titles that state the point force the story before you touch PowerPoint.

05Explain it back, then question it

Explain this document to me as if I just joined the team: [paste]. Then list the five questions I should ask before agreeing to it, and the one assumption it relies on most.

Why it works: you get the summary and the skepticism in one pass, before the meeting rather than after.

06The pre-mortem

Pretend it is six months from now and [project] has failed. Write the three most likely post-mortems in two paragraphs each, then for each one, the single action we would wish we had taken this month.

Why it works: imagining failure as already happened produces far more honest risks than "what could go wrong?"

07Spreadsheet formula, explained

In Excel I have columns [A: ..., B: ..., C: ...]. I want [the result in plain words]. Write the formula, explain each part in one line, and tell me the two ways it could silently give a wrong answer.

Why it works: the "silently wrong" line catches blanks, text-as-numbers and off-by-one ranges.

08Customer reply that keeps the customer

Draft a reply to this customer message: [paste]. Acknowledge the specific problem in the first sentence, explain what happened without excuses, offer a concrete next step with a date, under 120 words, warm and plain, no "we apologize for any inconvenience".

Why it works: structure plus a word budget removes the padding customers hate.

09Learn it in ten minutes

Teach me [topic] in ten minutes for a [my role]. The five ideas that matter most, one worked example, the mistake beginners make, and three questions I can ask an expert to sound like I did my homework.

Why it works: it asks for the shape of the subject, not a textbook, and ends with questions you can actually use.

10The Friday review

Here is my calendar and task list for the week: [paste]. Tell me what moved the needle toward [my top goal], what consumed time without moving it, and the three things to drop, delegate or shorten next week. Be blunt.

Why it works: an outside view of your own week is the cheapest productivity gain there is.
